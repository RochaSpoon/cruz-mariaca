## The short version

The Church has not issued a ruling on a genre. The Catechism defines pornography as displaying "real or simulated sexual acts" to third parties and warns against media that "go too far" (CCC 2354, 2523). Erotica written to arouse falls on the wrong side of that concern.

## What the Church teaches

"Pornography consists in removing real or simulated sexual acts from the intimacy of the partners, in order to display them deliberately to third parties. It offends against chastity because it perverts the conjugal act, the intimate giving of spouses to each other" (CCC 2354).

It "immerses all who are involved in the illusion of a fantasy world" (CCC 2354).

Modesty "protests... against the solicitations of certain media that go too far in the exhibition of intimate things" (CCC 2523).

Paul says to think about what is pure and lovely (Philippians 4:8).

A love story is not the same as erotica. The question for each book is what it is for, and what it does to you.

## Scripture

> "For the rest, brethren, whatsoever things are true, whatsoever modest, whatsoever just, whatsoever holy, whatsoever lovely, whatsoever of good fame, if there be any virtue, if any praise of discipline, think on these things."
*Philippians 4:8 (Douay-Rheims)*

Paul's test is practical. The same book can pass or fail depending on what it does to a particular reader.

## Why this matters today

Many romance novels are tame. Others are explicit and are written to arouse. The Church's concern is with the second kind.

Since the Catechism's definition mentions "displaying" acts, some argue that writing is different. The more common view is that arousal-driven material has the same effect, and that prudence applies.

## What's at stake

If it trains you to want fantasy over real people, it is working against you.

## How to live it

- **Ask** what a book is doing in you.
- **Put it down** if it is.
- **Choose** stories about real love.
- **Talk** to a priest if it has become a habit.
