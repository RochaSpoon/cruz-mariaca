## The short version

Goodbye is a contraction of "God be with ye." Dictionaries date the contraction to the 1500s, and the shift from "God" to "good" is explained by words like good day.

## What the Church teaches

Merriam-Webster describes goodbye as an alteration of "God be with you." Etymonline traces it to a contraction of "God be with ye," shaped by phrases such as good day and good evening.

Wiktionary traces intermediate forms such as godby and God be wi' you.

A viral claim that goodbye came from a misread abbreviation is described by dictionary sources as an oversimplification.

I could not fetch dictionary entries for other words in time for this lesson. Words like holiday (holy day) are widely cited, but I have not verified them here, so they are left out.

## Scripture

> "And behold, he came out of Bethlehem, and said to the reapers: The Lord be with you. And they answered him: The Lord bless thee."
*Ruth 2:4 (Douay-Rheims)*

Boaz greets the reapers with "The Lord be with you" and they answer "The Lord bless thee." Greetings of this form are old.

## Why this matters today

Many people say goodbye without knowing it once carried a blessing.

Viral etymology posts are often wrong. Check a dictionary before repeating one.

## What's at stake

This is a light lesson. It is here to show that the habits of Christian speech left marks in ordinary English.

## How to live it

- **Look up** a word in a real dictionary.
- **Be** careful with viral etymology claims.
