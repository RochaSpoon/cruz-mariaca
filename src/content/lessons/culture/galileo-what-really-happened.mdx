## The short version

Galileo was brought before the Inquisition in 1616 and tried again in 1633. John Paul II addressed the Pontifical Academy of Sciences on the case on October 31, 1992.

## What the Church teaches

The Catholic Encyclopedia says that in 1615-16 Galileo was "interrogated before the Inquisition," which declared the Copernican system "scientifically false, and anti-Scriptural or heretical" and told him to renounce it (Catholic Encyclopedia, "Galileo Galilei").

In 1632 he published a dialogue in which "a Ptolemist is utterly routed and confounded by two Copernicans," and the authorities took it as a challenge (Catholic Encyclopedia, "Galileo Galilei").

The article says the "E pur si muove" story is "an acknowledged fiction" (Catholic Encyclopedia, "Galileo Galilei").

It says the sentence of 1633 did "not receive the pope's signature" and made no formal decree on the subject. The author argues no pope bound the Church to geocentrism as a doctrine of faith (Catholic Encyclopedia, "Galileo Galilei").

John Paul II addressed the participants of the plenary session of the Pontifical Academy of Sciences on October 31, 1992, on the case. I could not retrieve its full text for this lesson.

## Why this matters today

Galileo is often used to claim that faith and science are at war. The case was complicated by personalities, politics and arguments about how to read Scripture.

The Church did not teach geocentrism as a dogma. The Church also made a serious mistake in how it handled the case. Both are true.

## What's at stake

The Church teaches that faith and reason do not conflict. The Catechism says methodical research, "provided it is carried out in a truly scientific manner and does not override moral laws, can never conflict with the faith" (CCC 159).

## How to live it

- **Read** an account by a historian of science, not just a polemic.
- **Hold** both: the case was a failure, and it was not a doctrinal definition.
