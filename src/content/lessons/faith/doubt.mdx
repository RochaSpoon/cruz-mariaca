## The short version

Having questions is not the same as losing faith. The Catechism distinguishes involuntary doubt, which is hesitation and difficulty, from voluntary doubt that refuses to believe (CCC 2088).

## What the Church teaches

"Voluntary doubt about the faith disregards or refuses to hold as true what God has revealed and the Church proposes for belief. Involuntary doubt refers to hesitation in believing, difficulty in overcoming objections connected with the faith, or also anxiety aroused by its obscurity" (CCC 2088).

The Catechism also says that "faith is certain. It is more certain than all human knowledge because it is founded on the very word of God who cannot lie." It adds that "revealed truths can seem obscure to human reason and experience" (CCC 157).

"Faith seeks understanding." A believer "desires to know better the One in whom he has put his faith" (CCC 158).

The first commandment asks us to "nourish and protect our faith with prudence and vigilance" (CCC 2088).

## Scripture

> "And Jesus saith to him: If thou canst believe, all things are possible to him that believeth. And immediately the father of the boy crying out, with tears said: I do believe, Lord: help my unbelief."
*Mark 9:22-23 (Douay-Rheims)*

The father in Mark 9 says, "I do believe, Lord: help my unbelief." It is a short prayer many people use.

## Why this matters today

Many people think doubt means the end of faith. The Catechism does not say that.

Even the apostles had it: "some doubted" (CCC 644). The question is what you do with it.

## What's at stake

Doubt that is ignored tends to harden. Doubt that is taken seriously often leads to deeper faith.

## How to live it

- **Write down the exact question.**
- **Look for the best answer** from a thoughtful believer, not the weakest.
- **Pray the father's prayer** in Mark 9.
- **Keep the practices** (prayer, Mass) while you ask.
