## The short version

The Church says palliative care "is a special form of disinterested charity" and "should be encouraged." Pain relief may be used "even at the risk of shortening their days," if death is not willed (CCC 2279).

## What the Church teaches

"The use of painkillers to alleviate the sufferings of the dying, even at the risk of shortening their days, can be morally in conformity with human dignity if death is not willed as either an end or a means, but only foreseen and tolerated as inevitable" (CCC 2279).

"Palliative care is a special form of disinterested charity. As such it should be encouraged" (CCC 2279).

Samaritanus Bonus (2020) has a section on palliative care and also teaches that those who ask for death are usually asking for help: "the pleas of gravely ill people who sometimes ask for death are not to be understood as implying a true desire for euthanasia; in fact, it is almost always a case of an anguished plea for help and love."

It adds that "palliative care in itself is not enough unless there is someone who 'remains' at the bedside of the sick."

## Why this matters today

Many people fear dying in pain, and that fear is behind some of the pressure for euthanasia. Good palliative care answers it.

Hospice also supports families. The Church treats this as a work of mercy (CCC 2447).

## What's at stake

Dying well means pain managed and someone there. The Church asks for both.

## How to live it

- **Learn** what hospice offers before you need it.
- **Visit** the dying.
- **Say** what you want and need.
- **Stay.**
