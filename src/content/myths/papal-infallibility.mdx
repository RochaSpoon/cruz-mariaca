## The claim

> "The pope is infallible, so Catholics think he can never be wrong."

> "So everything the pope says is the voice of God?"

## Why people believe it

The word <Term id="infallibility">infallibility</Term> sounds absolute, and it is often explained badly, even by Catholics. Headlines also treat any statement by a pope as a pronouncement. If infallibility meant that every papal sentence was beyond question, then the history of popes who disagreed with each other would make the idea absurd, and critics are right to say so.

## What the Church teaches

The doctrine is much narrower, and it takes some care to state.

<Teaching level="dogma" refs="First Vatican Council, Pastor Aeternus, chapter 4">
When the pope speaks "from the chair" (ex cathedra), as shepherd and teacher of all Christians, and defines a doctrine of faith or morals to be held by the whole Church, he is preserved from error. Such definitions are irreformable in themselves.
</Teaching>

<Teaching level="dogma" refs="CCC 891, Lumen Gentium 25">
The same gift belongs to the whole Church, and to the college of bishops when it teaches definitively together with the pope.
</Teaching>

The doctrine does not say the pope is sinless, that he cannot make mistakes in politics, science or administration, or that he can invent new revelation. The Church teaches that the gift exists to guard what was already handed on (CCC 891).

Catholic theologians also point out how rarely the power has been used. The usual examples are the two Marian definitions: the Immaculate Conception in 1854 and the Assumption in 1950. Whether other statements count is debated.

## Scripture

Catholics point to three passages. Jesus tells Simon that he is Peter, the rock, and gives him the keys (Matthew 16:18-19). Before the Passion Jesus tells Simon that he has prayed that his faith will not fail, and that he should strengthen his brothers (Luke 22:31-32). After the Resurrection Jesus tells Peter three times to feed his sheep (John 21:15-17).

Catholics read these as a special role for Peter in keeping the Church's faith. Protestants and Orthodox Christians read the same passages and do not find a definition of 1870 in them, and the disagreement is real.

## Magisterium

The First Vatican Council, an <Term id="council">ecumenical council</Term>, defined the doctrine in 1870 in the constitution Pastor Aeternus (chapter 4). The Second Vatican Council restated it and placed it alongside the infallibility of the bishops (Lumen Gentium 25). The Catechism summarizes both at CCC 891.

## Historical record

The most famous test case is Pope Honorius I (625-638), who wrote letters that seemed to support a view of Christ's will that the Church later rejected. The Third Council of Constantinople in 680-681 condemned him by name. Catholic scholars answer that his letters were private, not a definition ex cathedra, and the argument over what Honorius meant has gone on for centuries.

Before 1870 many Catholic bishops and theologians held that infallibility belonged to the Church as a whole, and some opposed the definition as unwise. A minority left the Church over it and formed the Old Catholic churches. These are part of the record too.

## The strongest remaining objection

An Orthodox theologian might say it this way.

*Orthodox Christians accept that Rome held a primacy of honor in the early Church. What we cannot accept is the claim that a pope's definitions are irreformable by themselves, and not because the Church receives them. In the first millennium doctrine was settled by councils, and the bishop of Rome took part in them but did not stand over them. Vatican I changed the shape of the Church by defining a power that was never used in this way in the early centuries.*

That is the strongest form of the objection, and it is not answered by quoting Matthew 16. Catholics reply that Vatican I did not invent the power but named something present in the early Church's appeals to Rome. Orthodox and Catholic scholars still disagree about what the evidence from the first thousand years shows. If you are weighing this question, read both accounts of the same events, and note how each side uses the same sources.
