## The claim

> "Catholics worship Mary."

> "Why pray to saints? Just pray to God."

> "Kneeling in front of a statue of Mary is idolatry."

## Why people believe it

From the outside it looks like worship. A Catholic kneels, lights a candle, says "Hail Mary" over and over, and sings hymns that praise her. Mary is called Queen and Mother and Advocate. If someone did those things for a human being, you would call it devotion at the very least.

Many Protestants and Orthodox Christians take the first commandment seriously, and they are right to. If you were raised to believe that worship belongs to God alone, then you will notice anything that looks like worship pointed somewhere else. That is a good instinct, and nothing about it is petty.

There is also a problem with the word. In older English, "worship" meant honor in general. The 1549 Book of Common Prayer has the groom say to the bride, "with my body I thee worship." Even the Lutheran Augsburg Confession of 1530 titles its article on the saints "Of the Worship of Saints" in its English form. So some of this is a clash of vocabulary, and some of it is a real disagreement. It helps to pull the two apart.

And yes, there have been excesses. Popular devotion has sometimes gone further than Church teaching allows, and the Council of Trent said so in its own decree on the saints.

## What the Church teaches

The Church teaches four things, and they carry different weight.

<Teaching level="dogma" refs="Second Council of Nicaea (787), CCC 2096, CCC 2132">
Adoration belongs to God alone: Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Honor given to Mary, the saints and holy images is a different act, called veneration. The two are different kinds of act.
</Teaching>

<Teaching level="dogma" refs="Council of Ephesus (431), CCC 495">
Mary is the Mother of God. The title is a statement about Jesus before it is a statement about her. The one she bore is God the Son, so she is the mother of God in the flesh. It does not say she existed before God or gave him his divinity.
</Teaching>

<Teaching level="authoritative" refs="Council of Trent, Session 25, CCC 956">
The saints in heaven are alive in Christ and pray for the Church. Christians may ask for their prayers, in the same way they ask a friend or a pastor to pray for them. Trent calls this good and useful.
</Teaching>

<Teaching level="authoritative" refs="Lumen Gentium 62, CCC 969-970">
Titles like Advocate and Mediatrix are applied to Mary, and the Church says they take nothing away from Christ, who is the one mediator. Her role depends entirely on his.
</Teaching>

A Catholic is not taught that Mary is a goddess, that she is a fourth person alongside the Trinity, or that she saves anyone by her own power (CCC 970).

## Scripture

Catholics and Protestants agree on the starting point. Worship is for God alone.

<Scripture cite="Matthew 4:10" checked>
Then Jesus saith to him: Begone, Satan: for it is written, The Lord thy God shalt thou adore, and him only shalt thou serve.
</Scripture>

The New Testament shows people refusing worship for the same reason. Peter lifts Cornelius from his knees (Acts 10:25-26). An angel stops John from bowing to him (Revelation 22:8-9).

So where do Catholics find a place for honoring Mary? In the text itself. Mary says it before anyone else does:

<Scripture cite="Luke 1:48" checked>
Because he hath regarded the humility of his handmaid; for behold from henceforth all generations shall call me blessed.
</Scripture>

Gabriel greets her as "full of grace" and Elizabeth calls her "the mother of my Lord" (Luke 1:28, 1:43). Catholics read these as the root of honoring her.

On asking others to pray, Paul does it all the time and James tells believers to do the same.

<Scripture cite="James 5:16" checked>
Confess therefore your sins one to another: and pray one for another, that you may be saved. For the continual prayer of a just man availeth much.
</Scripture>

The verse Protestants most often raise against this is 1 Timothy 2:5:

<Scripture cite="1 Timothy 2:5" checked>
For there is one God, and one mediator of God and men, the man Christ Jesus.
</Scripture>

Catholics point out that it sits right after Paul urges "supplications, prayers, intercessions" for all people (1 Timothy 2:1). If asking another believer to pray for you took away from Christ's role, Paul would be contradicting himself in the space of four verses. At Cana, Mary asks her son for help and tells the servants, "Whatsoever he shall say to you, do ye" (John 2:1-5).

Catholics also read Hebrews 12:1, with its "cloud of witnesses," and Revelation 5:8, where the elders offer the "prayers of saints" before the throne, as evidence that those in heaven are involved with the Church on earth. Jesus calls God "not the God of the dead, but of the living" (Luke 20:38).

## Magisterium

The Council of Ephesus in 431 affirmed that Mary is Theotokos, "God-bearer." The point was to protect the truth about Christ.

The Second Council of Nicaea in 787 drew the line this entry is about. Honor may be given to the saints and to holy images, but adoration is owed to God alone. The Catechism restates this at CCC 2132.

The Council of Trent, in its decree of 1563, taught that it is good to ask the saints to pray, and it also told bishops to remove superstition and abuse.

The Second Vatican Council gave Mary a chapter of its own in Lumen Gentium. Paragraph 66 says that the honor given to her differs in kind from the adoration given to God, and paragraph 62 says her titles take nothing from Christ. The Catechism gathers all of this at CCC 969-971.

## Historical record

The earliest known prayer to Mary is the *Sub tuum praesidium*, "Under your protection." It survives on a Greek papyrus now in the John Rylands Library in Manchester. Scholars disagree about its date. Some place it in the third century, and others later.

The fourth century gives a useful check. Epiphanius of Salamis wrote against a group in Arabia, the Collyridians, who offered cakes to Mary. He held that Mary should be honored and that no one should worship her. So the line between honor and worship was being drawn long before Trent.

In the East, the Orthodox accept Nicaea II as the seventh ecumenical council. They honor Mary and the saints and make the same distinction between veneration and adoration.

The Reformers did not all land in one place. Luther wrote a commentary on the Magnificat in 1521 that treats Mary with real warmth, and the Augsburg Confession lets Lutherans remember and imitate the saints while rejecting prayer to them. The Reformed churches went further and rejected any religious honor for the saints, as the Westminster Confession does.

## The strongest remaining objection

Here is the objection as a thoughtful Reformed Christian would put it.

*The Westminster Confession says religious worship is to be given to God alone, "not to angels, saints, or any other creature" (21.2). It also holds that God has fixed the way he is to be worshipped, and has not left it to us to add to it (21.1). The Bible shows believers praying to God and asking living believers to pray for them. It never shows anyone asking the dead. Your distinction between honor and adoration may be clear on paper. In a crowded church, with candles lit and a prayer addressed to Mary by name, it is hard to hold in practice.*

That is a serious objection, and part of it should be conceded.

It is true that no passage of Scripture shows a believer asking a departed saint to pray. Catholics do not claim there is one. They argue from what Scripture does say about the dead being alive in God, about the saints being joined to the Church, and about intercession not competing with Christ's mediation. They also rest on the fact that Christians prayed this way very early on, and they read that practice as the Church growing into what it received. Protestants read the same facts and come out elsewhere, because they hold that the Bible alone sets the limit for worship.

It is also true that the line can blur in practice. Trent said so. The Church does not claim every Catholic always gets it right.

The deeper disagreement is about authority. Can the Church, guided by tradition, develop practices beyond what Scripture explicitly commands? Catholics say yes, and Reformed Christians say no. Whether Mary is worshipped depends on that question more than on anything about candles or statues.
